## Practitioner Summary
A critical Tennessee Supreme Court decision defining the scope of civil "malicious harassment" (T.C.A. § 4-21-701) and its application to local governments. The Court established that a county can be held vicariously liable for the civil rights intimidation committed by its deputies or employees under the doctrine of *respondeat superior*.

## Key Practice Points
* **Derivation of Elements**: The elements of a civil malicious harassment claim are directly derived from the criminal offense of "civil rights intimidation" (T.C.A. § 39-17-309).
* **Individual Liability**: An employee of a government agency can be sued in their individual capacity for malicious harassment.
* **County Vicarious Liability**: A city or county government does not have immunity from these claims; it may be found civilly liable for malicious harassment violations committed by its agents or law enforcement officers if the acts occurred within the course and scope of their employment.

## Case Facts & Disposition
A Robertson County deputy engaged in a high-speed pursuit of a suspected intoxicated driver. After the suspect vehicle stopped and its occupants fled into the woods, officers stopped two African American men (Washington and Carr) who were in a nearby yard. The men were held at gunpoint, sprayed with mace, chased by a police dog, and handcuffed before being released when officers realized they were not the suspects. 

The men sued Robertson County, the City of Springfield, and the officers, alleging civil malicious harassment under the Tennessee Human Rights Act. The U.S. District Court certified three questions to the Tennessee Supreme Court. The Court accepted the questions and ruled that individuals can be held liable for malicious harassment, and crucially, that local governments can be held liable for their officers' actions under the traditional doctrine of *respondeat superior*.
