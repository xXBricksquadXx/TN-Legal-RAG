## Practitioner Summary
The Tennessee Supreme Court held that handwritten notes found at a death scene and confiscated by a municipal police department during an investigation are public records under the TPRA. The Court established that an individual officer's intent (e.g., taking items for "safekeeping") does not dictate public record status; the totality of the circumstances must determine if records were received in connection with official business.

## Key Practice Points
* **Totality of Circumstances Test**: Determining whether a document is a public record relies on whether it was received in connection with the transaction of official business, evaluated by all facts rather than an individual officer's subjective intent.
* **Broad Construction Mandate**: Confirming statutory directives, the Court reinforced that the Public Records Act must be broadly construed to give the highest possible public access, prioritizing transparency over restrictive investigative custody.
* **Use of Government Property**: Handing personal property or notes over to investigative custody during routine official tasks (such as drafting scene reports, photography, or processing) integrates those items directly into the public record domain.

## Case Facts & Disposition
Following the shooting death of State Representative Ted Ray Miller, Knoxville Police Department officers confiscated three handwritten notes found in plain view on a kitchen counter. KPD investigators used the notes to corroborate their conclusion of suicide and close the file, subsequently providing copies to the FBI and returning the originals to the family's attorney. The Knoxville Journal Corp. and news directors requested access to inspect the notes, which the city and the widow resisted. 

The Supreme Court of Tennessee reversed the Court of Appeals, affirming the Chancellor's ruling that the notes were public records received in connection with official municipal business, and ordered the documents unsealed for public inspection.
