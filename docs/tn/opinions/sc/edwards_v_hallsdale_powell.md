## Practitioner Summary
The definitive Tennessee Supreme Court decision differentiating between a temporary nuisance (governed by the GTLA) and a permanent taking of property (Inverse Condemnation). This distinction is critical for county liability because inverse condemnation bypasses GTLA damage caps and the 12-month statute of limitations.

## Key Practice Points
* **Nuisance (GTLA) vs. Taking**: If an interference with private property (like a sewer backup or flooding) is temporary and can be abated/fixed with reasonable expenditure, it is a tort (nuisance/negligence) subject to the GTLA's protections.
* **Inverse Condemnation Threshold**: If the governmental action causes permanent or recurring damage that essentially destroys the property's value or utility, it constitutes a "taking" requiring compensation under T.C.A. § 29-16-123.
* **No Cap on Takings**: Inverse condemnation claims are constitutional actions and are not shielded by GTLA sovereign immunity or statutory damage caps.

## Case Facts & Disposition
Property owners sued a local utility district after raw sewage backed up into their home on multiple occasions. The plaintiffs filed claims under both the GTLA (for nuisance) and under eminent domain statutes for inverse condemnation. The trial court dismissed the inverse condemnation claim. The Supreme Court clarified the boundary: because the utility district had the engineering capacity to repair the sewer lines and abate the backups, the damage was temporary. Therefore, the plaintiffs were limited to recovering under the GTLA for negligence/nuisance and could not sustain a claim for inverse condemnation (a permanent taking).
