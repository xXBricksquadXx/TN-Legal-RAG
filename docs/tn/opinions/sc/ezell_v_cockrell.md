## Practitioner Summary
The Tennessee Supreme Court established that the "public duty doctrine" survived the enactment of the Tennessee Governmental Tort Liability Act (GTLA). The Court held that police officers owe a duty to the public at large, not to specific individuals, shielding them from negligence suits unless a "special duty" was affirmatively created.

## Key Practice Points
* **Public Duty Doctrine**: Shields public employees from suits for injuries caused by the breach of a duty owed to the public at large (e.g., failing to arrest a drunk driver).
* **Special Duty Exception**: A special duty of care exists only when: (1) officials affirmatively undertake to protect the plaintiff and the plaintiff relies on it; (2) a statute specifically authorizes a cause of action for a particular class; or (3) the plaintiff alleges intent, malice, or reckless misconduct.
* **Simple Negligence Under § 1983**: Simple negligence causing unintended loss or injury does not constitute a violation of the Due Process Clause actionable under 42 U.S.C.A. § 1983.

## Case Facts & Disposition
Kimberly Ezell, injured by an intoxicated driver, sued the City of Elkton and its police chief, alleging the chief was negligent for failing to arrest the driver (who had offered to drive an intoxicated patron home from a bar) despite knowing the driver was intoxicated. An hour after interacting with the chief, the driver collided head-on with Ezell’s truck, killing her husband.

The trial court dismissed the action for failure to state a claim, relying on the public duty doctrine. The Court of Appeals affirmed. The Supreme Court of Tennessee affirmed the dismissal, ruling that the GTLA did not abolish the common-law public duty doctrine and that the facts did not support a "special-duty" exception because the police chief had no affirmative contact or undertaking to protect Ezell.
