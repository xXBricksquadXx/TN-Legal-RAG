## Practitioner Summary
The landmark Tennessee Supreme Court ruling that abolished the traditional "open and obvious" defense as an absolute bar to recovery in premises liability cases. The Court established that known or obvious dangers must be analyzed under the comparative fault framework, fundamentally altering how local governments defend slip-and-fall claims under T.C.A. § 29-20-204.

## Key Practice Points
* **Abolition of Absolute Bar**: A plaintiff is no longer automatically barred from recovering damages just because the hazard (e.g., a cracked sidewalk, an uneven paver) was "open and obvious."
* **Duty-Risk Analysis**: Even if a danger is obvious, a governmental entity still owes a duty of care if it can reasonably foresee that a person might be distracted, forget the hazard, or fail to protect themselves.
* **Comparative Fault Application**: The obviousness of the danger is now a factor weighed by the jury when assigning comparative fault (i.e., comparing the plaintiff's negligence in failing to look against the county's negligence in failing to repair). If the plaintiff's fault is 50% or greater, they cannot recover.

## Case Facts & Disposition
A 68-year-old woman tripped and fell on an uneven deviation between brick pavers and a concrete sidewalk outside the Savannah City Hall. The city argued it owed no duty because the hazard was "open and obvious" in broad daylight. The trial court found the city 70% at fault for installing and maintaining a known hazard. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding the plaintiff was at least 50% at fault because she failed to look. The Supreme Court clarified the law, stating that the open and obvious nature of a defect does not automatically eliminate a property owner's duty to maintain a safe premises, but rather is a key component of the comparative fault assessment.
